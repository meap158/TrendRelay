# Editorial and batch quality rules

A layer on top of the mechanical workflow, not a replacement for it.
`campaigns.fill-needs-copy` and `campaigns.add-post-with-media` decide **which
tools to call, which fields are missing, and when a batch is finished**. This
decides **whether the writing is any good**.

Read this before writing the first caption of a run. Where the two conflict,
the mechanical SOP wins on process and this one wins on wording.

## 0. Ask before you write, if the run needs it

Fetching this SOP does not oblige you to start writing immediately. When
something here cannot be answered from live MCP context, ask the operator
rather than guessing - once, in a batch, before the run rather than after
fifty posts have inherited the guess.

Worth asking about when the context does not settle it:

- The **mix** this batch should aim for, if the campaign is not purely
  commercial - see §2 and §7.
- Whether a **product is genuinely relevant** to a clip you cannot read well.
- Whether a claim in a source caption is **verified** - see §10.
- Whether a **first comment, title, thread, or disclosure** is wanted on this
  destination at all, when live context is ambiguous - see §14.
- Any **house voice** the operator has in mind that approved posts do not
  already demonstrate.

Do not ask what the live context already answers. A question whose answer is
one MCP call away is a question that wastes the operator's time.

---

## Part one - what the copy is for

### 1. Write media-first, not product-first

Start from what the viewer is actually seeing and hearing, and from whatever
emotion, joke, story, tension, or observation follows naturally from it.
Connect the product only when the fit is genuinely strong.

A weak product match must never distort the caption. If the only way to
mention the product is to bend the post around it, the post does not mention
the product.

### 2. Not every post is an advertisement

Monetization happens downstream - link placement, first comments, matching.
The feed itself still has to be worth following: entertainment, personality,
utility, opinion, stories, nostalgia, humour, conversation, and *occasional*
product relevance.

Commercial posts belong mixed among ordinary ones. A queue in which every
caption sells is a queue that reads as an ad account, whatever the individual
captions say.

### 3. Editorial relevance outranks matcher convenience

Never shape a caption to force affiliate matching. If the matcher starts
selecting a product because generic words happen to overlap - "home", "water",
"one", "good" - that is a coincidence of vocabulary, not evidence the product
belongs in the post.

Never stuff unrelated product keywords into hashtags for the same reason.

## Part two - variety across a batch

The single biggest failure mode of a large run is fifty posts that were
obviously written in one sitting by one process.

### 4. Vary length deliberately

A healthy queue contains very short one-liners, ordinary conversational
captions, and the occasional longer mini-story or observation. Fifty captions
of two sentences each is a tell, even when every one of them is well written.

### 5. Vary structure, not just wording

Changing vocabulary is not enough if every post is still
*observation → emoji → question*. That is one post written fifty times.

Rotate the **shape**: jokes, flat statements, mini-stories, hot takes,
questions, nostalgia, POVs, conversational reactions, practical tips,
punchlines, comparisons, plain mood captions.

### 6. Reuse the insight from duplicate media, never the copy

When several queued posts share a source asset or near-identical footage,
reuse your *understanding* of the clip - that is the expensive part - and
change the angle: humour for one, nostalgia for another, a straight
observation for a third, product adjacency for a fourth.

### 7. Distribute tone across the batch on purpose

Before starting a large queue, decide roughly how the posts spread across
buckets such as: humour · nostalgia · story · useful tip · opinion · mood ·
question · product-adjacent · community · short reaction.

Doing this deliberately is what stops a batch inheriting one mood simply
because it was processed in one sitting.

## Part three - voice

### 8. Write as a native speaker of the campaign language

Do not translate an English thought sentence-by-sentence into another
language. Use that language's own rhythm, slang, fragments, humour,
punctuation, and natural code-switching. The result should read as though it
was written in that language first, because it was.

### 9. Do not over-polish casual social copy

Slight fragments, colloquial phrasing, playful punctuation, imperfect
symmetry, and conversational wording usually read as more human than
grammatically pristine marketing prose. Good copy is not always formal copy.

### 10. Do not force engagement bait

Questions should arise because the post invites one. Avoid ending every
caption with the same appeal - "What about you?", "Anyone else?", "Drop a
comment". Sometimes the strongest post is a statement people want to react to
on their own.

When you do invite discussion, ask something specific enough to trigger a
memory, a preference, a disagreement, or a story. A conversation hook, not
homework.

## Part four - honesty

### 11. Preserve uncertainty when the source is questionable

Do not repeat claims from a scraped source caption as fact: "last ever
performance", earnings figures, relationship rumours, safety guarantees,
dramatic results. Reframe around the visible media, the mood, or a general
observation unless the claim is verified.

### 12. Handle money, work, health, safety, and results carefully

Do not imply guaranteed income, guaranteed outcomes, zero risk, or universal
results. Prefer "depends on…", "can vary…", or simply drop the claim when the
post does not need it. Most posts do not need it.

### 13. Treat old media as old

When the media obviously refers to a past milestone, giveaway, event, or
moment, write it as a throwback rather than as something happening now. Never
restate a historical price, promotion, giveaway, or availability as current
unless it is confirmed to still be true - reviving an expired offer by accident
is a real cost to a real audience.

### 14. The source caption is context, not copy

The original scraped caption exists to tell you what the media is. It carries
no obligation. When it is awkward, sensationalised, misleading, culturally
odd, or simply irrelevant, write a better post around the asset instead.

## Part five - fields and destinations

### 15. Mention products selectively and naturally

Where a product genuinely fits: name the category or use case naturally, avoid
catalog-style product names, avoid an abrupt sales pivot, and do not add links
by hand where the campaign places them for you.

### 16. Hashtags support the post; they do not replace writing it

Use the campaign's required or default set where one is specified, and add a
small number of content-specific tags only where they earn their place.

### 17. Auxiliary fields and first comments

Before writing a first comment, bio hint, disclosure, topic, title, thread, or
post type, check what the destination actually does with it. When link placement
is set to "No affiliate link" (`none`), writing a first comment is optional and
explicitly accepted for supplementary details, sizing guidance, fabric/material notes,
or audience engagement prompts (never URLs) which are preserved with the post. On
destinations where first comments deliver links, write copy that complements the
affiliate link. Do not generate first comments purely out of habit where no
supplementary purpose exists.

### 18. Titles are operational; captions are editorial

Where a title is editable but not shown publicly, it is for the operator: it
identifies and organises the post in the queue. Do not write it as a second
social caption unless the platform genuinely exposes it to viewers.

---

## Part six - working through a batch

### 19. Use performance data as inspiration, not as a template

High-performing published posts reveal what the audience responds to - length,
humour, subject, storytelling, tension. Borrow the underlying principle. Do
not clone the phrasing; a feed of variations on one successful caption stops
working quickly.

### 20. Save effort on understanding, not on individuality

Reuse campaign context, media understanding, established hashtags, and
recurring themes freely - that is what makes a large run affordable. Spend the
extra context-fetching calls only where the media or title is genuinely
ambiguous, or where a field needs destination-specific knowledge.

What must not be saved on is the writing itself.

### 21. Repair a failed write immediately

When a write fails, fix that item now rather than skipping it quietly. Adjust
the wording that caused the refusal, retry the same id, and move on only after
it succeeds or after a hard failure you can describe. A silently skipped post
is worse than a failed one, because nothing will come back for it.

### 22. Judge the batch as a sequence

The overall feed matters more than any individual caption. Before declaring a
run finished, read it as a reader would - in order.

Does it read like fifty posts from one real page with range and a personality,
or like fifty isolated answers? Optimise for the first. That judgement is the
point of this entire layer.
